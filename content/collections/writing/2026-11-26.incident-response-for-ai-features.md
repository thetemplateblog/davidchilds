Most companies have a solid incident process for outages. Something breaks, an alert fires, someone gets paged, and there is a runbook. AI features fail differently. The service is up, the latency is fine, the error rate is zero, and the system is confidently telling customers something false, or leaking data from another user's documents, or being steered by text hidden in a web page.

Your existing incident process mostly still works. It just needs a few additions so these failures get noticed, routed and fixed.

## What counts as an AI incident

Agree on a definition before you need one. A practical list:

- **Harmful or wrong outputs at scale.** Incorrect advice, made-up policies, offensive content, especially when customers act on it.
- **Data exposure.** The system reveals personal data, confidential documents, or another user's information.
- **Manipulation.** Prompt injection or jailbreaks that make the system take actions or say things it should not.
- **Unfair outcomes.** Results that systematically disadvantage a group, often found through complaints rather than monitoring.
- **Silent quality drops.** A vendor updates a model and your feature gets worse without anything breaking.
- **Runaway cost.** A loop, an abuse pattern, or a bug that burns through your API budget.

Each of these needs a severity scale. The one you use for outages, based on how many users are affected and how badly, usually works with small changes.

## How you find out

Outages page you. AI incidents mostly do not, which is why detection is the weak spot. Use several sources:

- **User reports.** A "report this answer" button on AI features is the cheapest monitor you can build. Route those reports somewhere a person reads them every day.
- **Support tickets.** Tag tickets that mention the AI feature, and watch for spikes.
- **Automated checks on live traffic.** Run a sample of production outputs through the same checks as your evaluation suite: groundedness, policy violations, sensitive data in outputs.
- **Scheduled regression runs.** Run your test set against production daily, so a vendor model change shows up as a failing build.
- **Cost and usage alerts.** Sudden jumps in token usage are often the first sign of abuse or a loop.

## Kill switches come first

The most useful thing you can build before an incident is a way to turn the feature off, or down, without a deploy. Good options, from least to most drastic:

- Roll back to the previous prompt or model version.
- Turn off a risky capability, such as tool use, web browsing or file access, and keep the rest running.
- Show results only after a human review.
- Replace the feature with a fallback, such as plain search or a "contact support" message.
- Turn it off entirely.

These should be feature flags or config changes that the on-call engineer can make in minutes. If turning off an AI feature means a code change and a deploy, people will hesitate, and incidents run longer.

## Investigating needs the right logs

When someone reports a bad answer, you need to reconstruct what happened. That means being able to find, for a given request: the model and version, the system prompt version, the retrieved documents or tool results, the user input, and the output.

This is where logging decisions made months earlier pay off. If you only logged metadata, you will know a request happened but not why it went wrong. If you log full content, you need access controls and retention limits, because those logs are sensitive themselves. A common compromise is full content for a short window, with access restricted to the people who handle incidents.

## Fixing it properly

The immediate fix is often a prompt change or a blocked input. The lasting fix is making sure it cannot quietly come back:

1. Add the failing case to your evaluation set, so a future change that reintroduces it fails the build.
2. Check whether the same weakness exists in other AI features. Prompt patterns and retrieval setups get copied.
3. If it was a vendor change, decide whether to pin model versions and how you will test upgrades.
4. If customers acted on bad output, involve the right people early: support, legal, and whoever owns customer communication.

## Who needs to know

Some AI incidents have reporting duties. A data exposure may be a personal data breach with a legal deadline for notifying the regulator. Under the EU AI Act, providers of high-risk systems must report serious incidents to authorities. Your legal and privacy teams should be on the escalation list for AI incidents from the start, not found halfway through.

## Practise it

Run a tabletop exercise. Pick a realistic scenario, such as "the support assistant has been telling customers they are eligible for refunds they are not," and walk through it. Who notices? Who is paged? Can on-call turn it off? Can you find the conversations affected? Most teams find their gaps in the first fifteen minutes, which is much better than finding them during the real thing.
