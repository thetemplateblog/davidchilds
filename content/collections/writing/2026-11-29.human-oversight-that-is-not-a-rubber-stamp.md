"A human reviews every decision" is the most common control in AI risk assessments. It is also one of the least effective, at least as usually implemented. Put a person in front of a queue of AI recommendations that are right most of the time, give them a target for how many to get through, and within a few weeks they are approving almost everything. The human is in the loop. They just are not doing anything there.

This is not a character flaw in reviewers. It is a well-documented effect called automation bias: people tend to trust automated suggestions over their own judgment, especially when the suggestions are usually correct and the work is repetitive. If you want human oversight to actually catch problems, you have to design for it.

## Decide what the human is for

Oversight means different things, and each needs a different design:

- **Catching errors before they land.** The reviewer checks outputs and blocks bad ones. This needs time per item and the information to judge.
- **Making the decision.** The AI informs, the human decides. This needs the human to form a view, not just accept one.
- **Handling exceptions.** The system decides routine cases and sends uncertain or high-impact ones to a person. This needs a good way to decide which cases go to the person.
- **Monitoring the system.** Someone watches aggregate behaviour and can stop it. This needs dashboards and a kill switch, not a review queue.

Be explicit about which one you are building. "A human is involved" is not a design.

## Route the right cases to people

Reviewing everything at the same depth is how reviewers stop paying attention. Most systems are better served by sending people fewer, harder cases:

- **High impact.** Anything that denies someone a service, changes money, or affects their job goes to a person.
- **Low confidence.** Cases where the model's confidence is low, where several models disagree, or where retrieval found little support.
- **Unusual inputs.** Cases far from what the system usually sees.
- **A random sample of the rest.** A small percentage of routine cases, so you keep measuring how often the automation is wrong.

The random sample is the part teams skip, and it is the part that tells you whether the routing works.

## Design the review screen against rubber-stamping

How you present a case changes how people judge it. A few patterns help:

**Show the evidence, not just the answer.** Put the sources, the relevant input fields and the reasoning next to the recommendation. A reviewer who can only see "approve: 94% confidence" has nothing to check.

**Sometimes ask for a judgment first.** For decisions where independence matters, show the case and ask for the reviewer's view before revealing the AI's recommendation. It is slower, so use it for the cases that justify it.

**Make disagreeing cheap.** If overriding takes three clicks and a free-text justification while approving takes one, you are training people to approve. Give overrides structured reasons, so the data is useful too.

**Watch the pace.** If reviewers are measured only on throughput, they will optimize throughput. Track how often they override, how long they spend, and how they score on planted test cases.

## Test the reviewers

You test the model, so test the oversight. Seed the review queue with known-bad cases at a low rate and measure how many get caught. If reviewers miss most of them, your human oversight control is not working, whatever the risk register says. Feed the results back as training, not blame. Missed cases usually point to a design problem in the screen or the workload.

## Give reviewers the power to stop things

Oversight is only real if the human can act on what they see. Reviewers should be able to flag a pattern, not just a case, and that flag should reach someone who can pause or roll back the system. The EU AI Act makes this explicit for high-risk systems: people assigned to oversight must be able to understand the system's limits, stay aware of automation bias, override outputs, and stop the system.

## Write down what oversight cannot do

Finally, be honest in your risk assessment about what the human control covers. A reviewer seeing one case at a time will not spot bias that only shows up in aggregate. A reviewer working at speed will not catch subtle factual errors. Those risks need other controls, such as fairness monitoring, evaluation suites and sampling audits. Human oversight is a strong control when it is designed for a specific job. It is a weak one when it is used to make everything else look safe.
