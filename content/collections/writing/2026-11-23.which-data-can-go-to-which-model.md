The most common AI policy question engineers get is not about bias or regulation. It is "can I paste this into the model?" Customer records, source code, contracts, incident notes. If your company has no clear answer, people either stop using useful tools or, more likely, use them anyway and hope.

A good AI data policy answers that question quickly, in terms people already understand, and is enforced by the platform rather than by memory.

## Start from the classification you already have

Most companies already classify data, usually something like public, internal, confidential and restricted. Do not invent a new scheme for AI. Map the existing levels to the kinds of model access you offer.

The model access tiers usually look like this:

- **Consumer tools on personal accounts.** No contract, and data may be retained or used for training. Public data only.
- **Enterprise vendor agreements.** A contract that rules out training on your data, sets retention limits, and covers security terms. Usually fine for internal and most confidential data.
- **Cloud-hosted models in your own account.** Models run through your cloud provider, inside your network controls, under your existing cloud agreement. Often approved for more sensitive data.
- **Self-hosted models.** Running entirely on infrastructure you control. Needed for the most restricted data, if you allow it at all.

Then the policy becomes a small table: for each data level, which tiers are allowed. People can remember a table. They cannot remember a twelve-page document.

## Questions to ask every vendor

The contract and the vendor's settings decide which tier a service belongs in. For each provider, get clear answers to:

- **Training.** Is our data used to train or improve models? Is that off by default, or do we have to opt out?
- **Retention.** How long are prompts and outputs kept? Is there a zero-retention option, and does it apply to every feature or only some?
- **Abuse monitoring.** Are prompts stored for abuse review, and can humans at the vendor read them?
- **Location.** Where is data processed and stored? Does it matter for your customers' contracts?
- **Subprocessors.** Which other companies touch the data?
- **Features.** Do file uploads, memory, web browsing or third-party plugins change any of the above?

The last one catches people out. A provider can have strict terms for its API and looser ones for the chat product, or for features that store files.

## Personal data needs its own thought

Personal data brings data protection law into the picture, and the usual questions apply: what is the lawful basis, is the use compatible with why the data was collected, and have you told people? Two AI-specific points are worth adding.

First, minimize before you send. If the task is summarizing a support ticket, the model probably does not need the customer's name, email address and account number. Strip or replace them before the call, and restore them afterwards if needed.

Second, think about outputs, not just inputs. A model that summarizes customer conversations creates new records containing personal data. Those need retention and access rules too.

## Enforce it in the platform

A policy people have to remember is a policy that gets ignored when a deadline is close. Build it into the paths people already use:

- **Route model access through a central gateway or account,** so you know which tier every request goes to and can block unapproved providers.
- **Label routes and applications by the highest data level they are approved for.** A route approved for internal data should not quietly start receiving restricted data.
- **Scan for obvious sensitive data** such as card numbers, government IDs and credentials before requests leave your network. It will not catch everything, but it catches mistakes.
- **Make the approved option the easy one.** If the sanctioned tool is good and already logged in, people stop pasting into personal accounts.

## Write it for the person at the keyboard

The final test of an AI data policy is whether an engineer at 4pm on a Friday can answer "can I use this model with this data?" in under a minute. Put the table at the top. List the approved tools by name, with links. Give three or four concrete examples: "a customer's support ticket goes to the enterprise assistant, not the free chatbot." Name a channel where people can ask when they are unsure.

Then review it twice a year. Vendors change their terms, new tools show up, and the data people want to use keeps growing. A policy that was right last spring may be wrong now.
